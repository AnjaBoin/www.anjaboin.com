*Or: how it takes two countries to get one benefit.*

I have been a cross-border worker for almost my entire working life. I mostly lived in the Netherlands (apart from a short period when I lived in Düsseldorf for a year) and worked in Germany. Life as a cross-border worker is actually fairly simple, as long as you – like me – only work in one country at a time. You do have to deal with tax returns in both countries, but because you have no income in one of them, you are exempt there and end up paying your taxes in the other. So far, so good.

## Social security as a cross-border worker

Life became a lot more complicated when I fell ill for a long time and eventually became temporarily unable to work. Suddenly I was confronted with a whole collection of legal and administrative peculiarities that, fortunately, I had known nothing about until then. Within the European Union, as a cross-border worker you are in principle only covered by social insurance in one country at a time. That is the country you pay your contributions to, and therefore the country you have to turn to for your social benefits. That sounds simple enough, but there is one curious exception: depending on your work situation, you can even switch your country of social insurance from one day to the next. This happens, for example, to cross-border workers who work from home for a day. Extremely inconvenient, because the social security systems of the different countries differ quite a lot. I was yet to find out how much difference that can make.

## Sick pay and the problem with sick notes

In my case, I worked in Germany, had paid contributions in Germany for many years and was therefore dependent on the German social security system. My first encounter with it was through *Krankengeld* (sick pay). In Germany, *Krankengeld* is paid for a maximum of 78 weeks under certain conditions. In return, you have to hand in a sick note every month. And they are very strict about it: there must be absolutely no gap between consecutive sick notes. A single day without a valid sick note can already cause problems.

As a cross-border worker, finding a doctor willing to issue such a certificate turned out to be quite a challenge. After all, I live in the Netherlands, where my GP, my specialists and my entire medical file are. But the Netherlands was not responsible for my sick notes. On top of that, Dutch doctors are not allowed to issue German sick notes. So that was problem number one.

In the end, the UWV (the Dutch employee insurance agency) turned out to have a solution. You report sick to the UWV by phone, and they coordinate the sick note with the German *Krankenkasse* (health insurance fund). That should have settled everything. On paper, at least. In practice, Germany had meanwhile switched to electronic sick notes. And these apparently work flawlessly, as long as every single detail matches to the letter. If it says "Mrs Anja Boin" somewhere instead of "A. Boin", the sick note can end up in an administrative no man's land: a kind of digital holding pot for sick notes that cannot be matched to the right person straight away.

A member of staff at the *Krankenkasse* then has to look into it personally. And in my case, that happened not once, but five times. The result? The *Krankenkasse* refused to pay my *Krankengeld* and even threatened to stop the benefit altogether. After all, I had no valid sick note. The fact that one authority claimed to have sent the note properly and the other claimed never to have received it was apparently a minor detail. I could no longer get a word in. The UWV insisted the note had been sent, the *Krankenkasse* insisted nothing had arrived. End of story.

In the end, the so-called DVKA (*Deutsche Verbindungsstelle Krankenversicherung Ausland*, the German liaison office for health insurance abroad), a kind of interface between the *Krankenkasse* and foreign authorities, provided the solution. After a lot of emailing back and forth and a fair amount of administrative effort, the problem was solved. Five times the same mistake. You would almost think something was structurally not quite right somewhere.

## The next problem

But that was not the end of it. The *Krankenkasse* also turned out to be creative with my money in other ways. For instance, they tried to treat a one-off payment from a risk insurance policy as a monthly pension all of a sudden. That would have meant paying additional contributions on it. Under German law, this is apparently allowed in certain situations, and it is then up to the insured person to object if the *Krankenkasse* interprets the law very generously in its own interest. Which, in my case, it did.

My case has now been before the *Sozialgericht* (social court) for more than a year. In my view, it is not allowed to be done this way at all, but until the judge has ruled, I dutifully pay the disputed contributions every month. A kind of subscription to a legal disagreement, for which I get to foot the bill myself.

When that trick seemed to have an effect, the *Krankenkasse* tried another approach. They referred me to the medical service, because there were supposedly doubts as to whether I was "really ill". I found that rather remarkable. After all, every month I went for a check-up with a doctor contracted by that very same *Krankenkasse*. And the *Krankenkasse* already had detailed reports from specialists. But fine, apparently there was a need for an additional medical assessment.

The medical service wanted my complete file. And here, too, the rule is: as the insured person, you are responsible for providing all the information and keeping an eye on the procedure yourself. If you do nothing, you risk being declared fit for work for lack of medical evidence. Or being regarded as uncooperative, with your *Krankengeld* being stopped as a result. Apparently it is not enough to be ill. You also have to be able to prove it convincingly on paper if the *Krankenkasse* doubts your illness.

After this attempt had failed, and the medical service had confirmed that I was still ill after all, the *Krankenkasse* had one last trump card up its sleeve. Before I finally fell ill, I had completed a medical rehabilitation programme (*medizinische Rehabilitation*, also known as *Kur*). I had been discharged from it fit for work. What's more: between my rehabilitation and my actual sick leave, I had worked for several more weeks.

But suddenly the *Krankenkasse* claimed that I had already been ill when my rehabilitation began. That moved the start date of my illness forward by two months. And of course that also meant my *Krankengeld* would end two months earlier. This was by no means a harmless administrative correction. Had I done nothing, I would have lost two months of sick pay.

I had to lodge an objection (*Widerspruch*) no fewer than two times before they made a remarkable discovery: I had been discharged from rehabilitation fit for work at the time and had simply gone back to work afterwards. So the *Krankengeld* had to be paid for two more months. Oh, right. That had been known all along, and the *Krankenkasse* had had the documents showing that I was fit for work at the time for two years already. Quite remarkable how they then claim "just like that" that things were different. By the end of the maximum 78 weeks, I had had more than enough of this *Krankenkasse*.

## Between sick pay and disability pension

Unfortunately, that was not the end of it. I was still unable to work and recovering, which landed me at the next German counter. Germany has a strange gap between *Krankengeld* and the disability pension (*Erwerbsminderungsrente*). The *Deutsche Rentenversicherung* (German pension insurance) has considerable backlogs, so the assessment can sometimes take months. As a result, there can easily be four to ten months between the end of *Krankengeld* and the decision on the disability pension.

Fortunately, there is a scheme for this interim period. People waiting for their assessment can be temporarily supported by the *Agentur für Arbeit* (the German employment agency) and then receive unemployment benefit. And this is where it gets interesting.

You are assessed by a medical examiner, who has to determine whether you can still work. Not in your old profession, but on the general labour market. And the criteria for that are pretty broad. If, for example, you can still move one hand, you could in theory answer the phone. And if you can theoretically keep that up for six hours a day or longer, then according to these criteria you can be fully fit for work. Whether a job actually exists that you could do with your existing complaints is irrelevant.

So you are formally fit for work if the medical examiner believes you can work at least six hours in any job whatsoever. And that makes you unemployed. That is already a peculiar line of reasoning for people who have a job, including a permanent contract, but can no longer do it because of illness. Yet they are forced to register as unemployed, because not cooperating means (you guessed it) no benefit. And so, as a temporarily unfit pilot with a permanent employment contract, I suddenly had to register as unemployed just to keep any income at all. Welcome to the wondrous world of social security.

## Which country pays?

The unemployment benefit then turned out not to be straightforward either. The *Agentur für Arbeit* applied a rather simple rule: if you do not live in Germany, they do not pay benefits in principle. In their view, the country of residence was responsible in that case. So my application was initially rejected. Next, I knocked on the door in the Netherlands. But there, too, I got nowhere. After all, I still had an employment contract in Germany. Under Dutch social security law, Germany was therefore responsible. And so a remarkably efficient system emerged in which both countries pointed at each other and nobody was willing to pay.

In the end, legal proceedings were needed once again. After lengthy objection procedures (*Widerspruchsverfahren*), the Netherlands turned out to be right: as long as there is still a link with the country of employment in the form of an employment contract (even if that contract is dormant), the country of employment remains obliged to pay the unemployment benefit. After my third objection, the *Agentur für Arbeit* had to admit through gritted teeth that they did in fact owe me a benefit.

But even then, the administrative adventure was not over. The *Agentur* had found another way to keep my benefit as low as possible. Without checking with the tax office, which is actually standard practice in cases like this, they had simply placed me in tax class I (*Steuerklasse I*), assuming that as a cross-border worker I was in that tax class. That was simply an assumption on the part of that particular *Agentur für Arbeit*, and it was wrong. As someone treated as a resident taxpayer, I was entitled to a more favourable tax class. So once again I had to chase my paperwork to prove it. The difference? Almost 300 euros a month. Not exactly an amount you just leave on the table. Especially not when you depend on a benefit.

## Two countries, two verdicts

To my surprise, when I applied for the German *Erwerbsminderungsrente*, it turned out that I might also be entitled to a disability benefit from the Netherlands. This was because I had built up about ten years of work history in the Netherlands in the past, mainly through side jobs. That meant I might also be entitled to a Dutch benefit. And so I got to go through the medical mill in two countries at the same time. Had I not done so, I would have lost part of the benefit, because together the two countries pay one full benefit. My German work history alone would therefore only have earned me a partial disability pension.

This led to a particularly interesting outcome. Under European legislation, assessments of incapacity for work do not always match. In theory, you can be declared fit for work in one country and unfit for work in the other. In my case, that is exactly what happened. The Netherlands declared me fully and permanently unfit for work. I was granted a benefit until my retirement. Germany, on the other hand, declared me fully fit for work. So in one country I had to stop all work because I was not fit for it, and in the other I was expected to simply go to work. Two countries, two medical assessments and two completely different conclusions. I wondered how that was possible and who had come up with legislation like this. After all, it was about one and the same body.

The consequences were rather strange. In the Netherlands, working is penalised by reducing your benefit. Worse still: if you keep working, the benefit is withdrawn altogether. However, because of my limited work history, my Dutch benefit was so low that I actually had to work on top of it. There turned out to be no specific legal provision for this unusual situation. So the Netherlands may reduce my benefit if I work in Germany, even though I have officially been declared fully unfit for work in the Netherlands. And so you can end up being too ill to work in one country and healthy enough to get to work in the other. This also creates a strange difference compared with an employee who lives and works in the Netherlands: they would simply have received a full disability benefit and would not have been obliged to work.

## Reintegration

Because I did not want to sit at home while waiting to recover enough to work as a pilot again, I signed up for vocational rehabilitation (*berufliche Rehabilitation*) in Germany. This, too, turned out to be difficult. The *Deutsche Rentenversicherung*, which funds this programme, made quite a fuss about my cross-border status and the Dutch medical file I had to send in. Officially, there is European legislation stating that the authorities coordinate such matters with each other and confirm, among other things, the validity of medical documents. In practice, however, "cases" like mine are so rare that people on both sides of the border are not aware of it. So once again, everything was initially rejected and delayed. I had to wait more than ten months for the *Rentenversicherung* to approve it. Then things finally got moving, and I once again had a prospect of being able to work within my health limitations.

## Conclusion

By now I have gained a considerable amount of experience with the social security systems of both countries. It was not quite the international career I had in mind when I once started out as a pilot. But one thing I have learned: as a cross-border worker, you had better have a good dose of patience, a good filing system and a decent amount of perseverance. Because your rights only exist on paper; actually having them granted in the end is quite another challenge.

And perhaps most important of all: make sure you know your rights well yourself. Because although there are all kinds of authorities on both sides of the border to help you, in the end it often turns out to be your own responsibility to make sure they do their job properly. The European Union has done an excellent job of regulating the free movement of workers. However, the legislation does not cover every case. And the free movement of your benefits seems to need a bit more practice.
